# Immersion

Load this when self-checking or fixing "the reader falls out of the story" or "I can't get into it", or when designing a key high-immersion scene. The baseline (five layers: POV lock + sensory immersion + emotional resonance + situation design + error avoidance) lives in SKILL.md; this is the full expansion.

> Related: emotion-label → concrete-behavior rewriting in [Prose & Style](craft-prose-style.md); POV boundaries and the four distance gears in [POV & Distance](craft-pov-and-distance.md); micro-tension and emotional layers in [Emotion & Subtext](craft-emotion-and-subtext.md).

Immersion is never one trick — several layers work at once. Collapse any one of them and the reader "notices they're reading".

## 1. POV lock — let the reader live inside the protagonist's head

- Prefer third-person limited: the reader perceives only what the POV character perceives.
- First person has a strong voice but sacrifices multi-plotline flexibility; omniscience tends to lose immersion.
- **Never switch POV inside a scene**; switch at scene or chapter boundaries and lock the new character immediately.
- Choosing the POV: whoever is present and has the most at stake emotionally.

## 2. Sensory immersion — make the reader feel present

- At least three senses in every important scene, but **not all five** — pick the ones this scene needs.
- **Body before brain**: physiological response first, conscious judgment second.
- **Spatial anchoring**: one or two opening lines place the reader in the space (size, light, smell, available exits).
- Senses follow the character's attention: a frightened person hears first; a hungry person smells first.

## 3. Emotional resonance — make the reader's heart beat with the character's

- Replace abstract emotion with concrete behavior (see Emotion & Subtext).
- **"Ordinary-person moments"**: insert plain, real instants into grand plot — hands shaking while tying shoelaces before the final battle; the cold killer slowing his step past a flower shop.
- **Establish, then threaten**: make readers like a person, a thing, or a life first, then threaten it. No establishment means no loss.
- **Costs must be real**: mistakes have consequences, injuries leave marks, what is lost doesn't easily return.

## 4. Situation design — make immersion easy to enter

- **Start low**: the closer the opening situation is to ordinary emotional experience, the easier it is to inhabit.
- **Make choices hard**: readers inhabit the *process* of a painful decision, not the result of being brilliant.
- **Costs must be real**: every gain is paid for, and the reader can feel the price.
- **Capability match**: the protagonist must be able to *reach* the goal, or readers won't believe the stakes.

## 5. Micro-tension — keep even calm passages uneasy

Even without conflict, keep a small unease (an unanswered question, an oppressive detail, words contradicting actions, an approaching consequence). If three randomly sampled passages are all "very calm", the reader is on flat ground — more than three chapters of that and retention drops. See [Emotion & Subtext](craft-emotion-and-subtext.md).

## 6. Common immersion breakers

| Breaker | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Authorial explanation | the narrator explains the world | surface it through character POV, or scatter it into conflict |
| Emotion labels | emotion named directly | swap for body response and action |
| Omniscient warning | "little did he know this decision would change his life" | delete; let the reader experience it alongside the character |
| Character stupidity | dumb decisions to move the plot | check whether the motive is "the author needs it" |
| Coincidence overload | plot advanced by repeated luck | coincidence may create problems, never solve them |
| Over-long interior monologue | long blocks of thinking | distribute into action, dialogue, and sensation |
| Distance jumps | one passage intimate, next distant | see [POV & Distance](craft-pov-and-distance.md) |
| Terminology barrage | a wall of proper nouns up front | release setting gradually through conflict (see Opening Hooks) |

## 7. Immersion self-check (after each chapter)

- [ ] Does the reader know "who I am" (clear, stable POV)?
- [ ] What can be felt in this scene (enough senses, following the character's attention)?
- [ ] Do reactions read like a real person's (body first, judgment second)?
- [ ] Any authorial explanation convertible to character POV?
- [ ] Is the establish-then-threaten order in place?
- [ ] Do three consecutive passages lack all unease?

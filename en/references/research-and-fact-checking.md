# Research and Fact-Checking

Use this guide when fiction relies on real history, science, medicine, law, geography, culture, public figures, products, or platform policies.

## Triage claims by risk

- **High risk:** safety, medical or legal behavior, accusations about identifiable people, central historical events, and claims that determine the plot.
- **Medium risk:** profession-specific procedure, travel time, technology capability, cultural practice, or precise statistics.
- **Low risk:** incidental atmosphere that does not affect decisions or reputation.

Research high-risk claims first. If current rules, prices, roles, or policies may have changed, verify them from current sources.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer primary and authoritative sources: statutes, agencies, standards, platform policies, original research, official archives, maps, and direct documentation. Use reputable secondary sources for context and disagreement. Treat social posts and generated summaries as leads, not proof.

Record the claim, source, publisher, URL or citation, access date, conclusion, scope, and intended use in [the source log](../templates/source-log.md).

## Separate fact from invention

Label findings as verified, disputed, uncertain, or deliberately fictionalized. Preserve uncertainty where experts disagree. Never invent a citation, quote, statistic, or document.

When realism conflicts with story needs, change the fictional premise transparently, narrow the claim, or seek specialist review. For high-stakes professional guidance, fiction research is not a substitute for qualified advice.

## Check the draft

Search for precise dates, measurements, technical statements, quotations, real organizations, and claims about identifiable people. Trace each important claim to the source log. Paraphrase sources unless quotation is necessary and permitted.

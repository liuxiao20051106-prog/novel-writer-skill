# Character Building

Load this when designing a new character, revising an arc, or diagnosing "the character is flat", "the motive is foggy", "the side characters are tools". The baseline (five layers; want vs. need) lives in SKILL.md; this is the full expansion.

> Related: arcs against three-act structure in [Story Structure](craft-story-structure.md); voice and subtext in [Dialogue](craft-dialogue.md); emotion and defenses in [Emotion & Subtext](craft-emotion-and-subtext.md).

Lineage (paraphrased): McKee's "true character is revealed in choices under pressure" and the principle of antagonistic forces; K.M. Weiland's arc types; Maass on uniqueness, empathy, and motivation.

## I. Characterization ≠ true character

- **Characterization**: the sum of observable traits (looks, accent, job, habits, background).
- **True character**: **the choices made under pressure**. The greater the pressure, the more the choice exposes the person; a choice between good and evil isn't a choice — it requires no struggle.

So the way to write character is not to "introduce who they are" but to **keep putting them into dilemmas**. Readers remember choices, not adjectives.

## II. Five layers (shallow to deep)

1. **Archetype position**: idealist / cynic / survivor / guardian / seeker… (not a good-evil split)
2. **Outer + inner desire**: looks, habitual gesture, what they actually want
3. **Wound and defense**: what hurt them and how they protect themselves (coldness / placating / control / avoidance / attack) — **defenses are the source of the flaw**
4. **Contradiction**: mutually incompatible traits (a ruthless killer keeps a cat; a gentle mother tells a colossal lie)
5. **Arc of fate**: changed understanding through ordeal — not "getting stronger" but "recognizing oneself anew"

## III. Want vs. need

- **Want (surface goal)**: what the character believes they're after (revenge, money, winning someone's love).
- **Need (deep growth)**: what they actually lack and don't know they lack (to let go of hate, to learn trust).
- A good ending: they get the need at the cost of the want — or they get the want and discover it isn't what they wanted.

## IV. Three arc types

| Type | Trajectory | Fits |
|------|-----------|------|
| **Positive** | false belief → admit and change (cowardice → courage, selfishness → sacrifice) | most commercial fiction |
| **Negative** | refuse to change → corruption or ruin (vigilance → paranoia → isolation) | tragedy, villain POV, cautionary tales |
| **Flat** | the protagonist doesn't change; the world does (detectives, old-school heroes, the steadfast) | series, case-of-the-week, payoff-driven leads |

A flat arc isn't "no character work" — it means **the character is the constant and the cast and world change**, which requires a belief weighty enough to carry it.

## V. Four things that make a character stand up

- **Unique**: one detail nobody else has (a trade habit, a catchphrase, an obsession about something small).
- **Empathy**: one universal emotional lack (betrayed, wants to be seen, afraid of loss).
- **Motivational depth**: the motive traces back to a specific experience, not "that's just how he is".
- **Capability match**: abilities fit the genre's ceiling and must be *within reach* of the goal — otherwise readers won't buy it.

## VI. Speech fingerprint (the layer that gives you away fastest)

Give every important character a small table and check dialogue and interior monologue against it:

| Dimension | Range |
|-----------|-------|
| Sentence length | clipped / medium / long and winding |
| Vocabulary | literary / street / jargon / foreign words |
| Catchphrases and taboo words | yes/no, and which |
| Politeness | honorifics / equal / commanding |
| Information density | one thing per sentence / three meanings per paragraph |
| How emotion shows | direct / ironic / silent / deflected |

**Check**: cover the speaker names and read a group-dialogue passage — if you can't tell who's talking, the fingerprint isn't built.

## VII. Side characters and antagonists

- **Side characters have brains**: the stronger the opponent, the more a win weighs. Never make a villain stupid just to move the plot.
- **Clear function**: mirror (same situation, different choice) / contrast (opposite personality, same dilemma) / catalyst (forces the protagonist to decide) / theme-bearer.
- **The antagonist is the thematic opposite**: if the protagonist stands for freedom, the antagonist stands for control — not merely "the one blocking the hero".
- **Comprehensible antagonists**: "in their shoes, I might have too…" beats pure evil.
- Antagonists also get want vs. need, and **believe themselves the protagonist of their own story**.

## VIII. Relationship web

For each important pair, write one line of emotional essence (love / hate / guilt / admiration / jealousy) and a direction of change (trust → betrayal / enmity → respect). Relationships advance through shared experience and cost, never by authorial announcement.

## IX. Minimum character-card fields

Name / role / age / appearance keywords / want / need / wound & defense / speech pattern / current state (location, emotion, possessions, unfinished business) / relationship to protagonist / arc direction / taboo (what they would never do)

## X. Common faults

| Symptom | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Flat character | add contradiction plus one past they won't discuss |
| Foggy motive | trace it to one concrete event and show a corner of it in the text |
| Tool side character | give them a small goal that conflicts with the protagonist's |
| Everyone sounds alike | build the fingerprint table; read passages with names covered |
| Passive protagonist | give them at least one active choice per chapter |
| Sudden stupidity | check whether this decision serves "the author needs it" rather than "the character would choose it" |

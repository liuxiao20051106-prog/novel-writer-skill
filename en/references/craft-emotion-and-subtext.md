# Emotion & Subtext

Load this when a scene is "correct but unmoving", when characters name feelings instead of performing them, when readers don't ache or tense up, or when designing a major emotional scene. The hard rule (render emotion through body and action, never name it; every scene needs micro-tension) lives in SKILL.md.

> Related: [Prose & Style](craft-prose-style.md), [POV & Distance](craft-pov-and-distance.md), [Webnovel Rhythm](craft-webnovel-rhythm.md).

Method lineage: Donald Maass's emotional layers and micro-tension, Angela Ackerman's body–internal–defense triad, and the standard dramatic practice of subtext (all paraphrased, not quoted).

## Three layers of emotion

Emotion has to be dug down layer by layer. Stopping at layer one is "naming feelings":

| Layer | What it is | Example |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **1. Label** | the name a reader could supply | he was angry / she was sad |
| **2. Body & behavior** | what the emotion looks like on a body | he gripped the cup until his knuckles whitened; his voice went low |
| **3. Deeper truth** | what the emotion is really afraid of | not being scolded — being called worthless again, the way his father used to |

**Method**: write layer 2 first (body before brain), then let the character or someone else reach layer 3. Avoid layer 1.

## Three columns instead of adjectives

When you catch "he was X", pick one or two details from each column — never all:

1. **Body signals**: shallow breath, sweaty palms, stomach dropping, neck tightening, voice sharpening, movement slowing or speeding
2. **Internal sensations**: time distortion (a second lasting forever), narrowed attention (staring only at the other's hands), thought loops (one phrase repeating)
3. **Psychological defenses**: changing the subject, joking, rationalizing, attacking, placating, going silent, pretending it's fine, stepping out for a smoke

**Key**: defenses are where character shows. Humiliated, one person hits back, one laughs along, one smashes something later.

## Micro-tension: keeping quiet scenes uneasy

The pull that keeps readers reading comes less from big conflicts than from "a small unease in every passage". Four tools:

1. **An unanswered question**: raise something the reader wants answered; delay the answer.
2. **Oppressive detail**: let the setting threaten (a dripping tap, an opponent checking his watch, someone edging closer).
3. **A character contradicting himself**: says he's fine, does the opposite.
4. **Imminent consequence**: a countdown or an approaching deadline (the train leaves in five minutes).

Check: sample three passages at random. If none of them carries any unease, the reader is walking on flat ground — three flat chapters in a row and you lose them.

## Designing the emotional curve

- Within a chapter: **opening state → rise/pressure → turn → landing**. The gap between landing and opening is the chapter's "emotional yield"; zero yield reads as wasted time.
- Across chapters: never peak the same emotion twice in a row (two grief-heavy chapters numb the reader). Use tight–easy–tighter.
- Before a big scene, apply pressure: make readers care (build a relationship or a stake), then threaten it, then release.
- Big payoff needs matching "frustration credit": the more chapters of suppression, the bigger the release — but small payoffs must be sprinkled in between.

## Subtext

Subtext = what the character says vs. what they actually want vs. what is being fought over in the relationship.

- Three layers run at once: **literal** (the topic), **intent** (what they want the other to do), **relation** (who is on top).
- Fastest method: write the real intent, then write a line that never mentions it.

| Real intent | On the nose | With subtext |
|-------------|-------------|--------------|
| I want you to stay | "Don't go." | "It's pouring. The umbrella's behind the door." |
| I hate that you betrayed me | "I hate you." | "Remember that bar where we first drank? Torn down years ago." |
| I'm testing what you know | "Do you know about this?" | "What time did you go to sleep that night?" |

**Taboo**: don't let a character say the whole truth, including in interior monologue. Leave 30% for the reader to guess.

## When naming the emotion is fine

- Low-value emotions you need to move past fast ("he was annoyed but let it go").
- Transitions and summarized time jumps.
- A first-person narrator deliberately self-mocking or ironic.

## Checklist

- [ ] Any named emotion that could become body/action?
- [ ] Is each major character's defense mechanism consistent and recognizable?
- [ ] In big scenes, did you make readers care before threatening it?
- [ ] Do three consecutive passages lack all unease?
- [ ] Does every key line carry intent beneath the literal?
- [ ] Is the chapter's emotional landing different from its opening (yield ≠ 0)?
